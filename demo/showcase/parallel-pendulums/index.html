Parallel pendulums

A 14-second video of two hanging bobs that share a string. Four points were tracked in the video, and the discovery agent was given only their unnamed coordinates. It returned equations of motion; the video shows those equations simulated on top of the real footage.

Tracked in the video.   The discovered model, simulated from the first frame only and never corrected from the data afterwards. Thin lines join each simulated knot to its bob.

Law found (horizontal motion of the two bobs)

ẍ1 = −25.4 − 35.28 (x1 − 507.1) + 3.55 (x2 − 704.7)
ẍ2 = −20.8 + 3.64 (x1 − 507.1) − 35.54 (x2 − 704.7)

x1, x2: horizontal position of the left and right bob, in video pixels; time in seconds. The agent wrote this as a first-order system (position and velocity as separate variables); it is shown here in the equivalent second-order form. The full 16-equation model is below.

What it means

  • Each bob is pulled back towards its rest position at a rate of about 35 s⁻², which alone would give a swing period of about 1.06 s.
  • Each bob is also pulled weakly, at about 3.6 s⁻², by the other one. The two coupling numbers were fitted independently and came out nearly equal.
  • Together these give two swing modes with periods of about 1.01 s and 1.11 s, which is why the swing passes back and forth between the bobs.
  • No friction term: adding velocity terms did not improve the fit over this 14 s clip.

How far to trust it

Tool's own confidence: low

The horizontal equations reproduce the video well and stayed the same to within 1–2% under a second fitting method. The vertical equations in the full model do not hold up, and everything was fitted and checked on this one clip.

Typical distance is the root-mean-square distance. "Share of swing" is the root-mean-square error divided by the standard deviation of that coordinate; 100% means no better than a fixed point. The frame is 1280 px wide and the bobs swing through roughly 100 px.

  • Horizontal motion stays in step. The simulated swing stays within one frame (0.02 s) of the real one for the whole clip. Its amplitude is 7–10% too large.
  • Vertical motion is not predicted. The bobs and knots move only a few pixels up and down, and the model's vertical error is about as large as that motion.

Limits

  • Same clip for fitting and checking. The model was fitted on these 704 frames, so the video shows how well it reproduces them, not how well it predicts a new swing.
  • Low confidence from the tool itself. Its assessment found extra terms the data favour, five variants that fit equally well, and candidate models that part ways after a single frame.
  • No independent review. The agent's built-in critic did not run, because the agent submitted with too few tool calls left.
  • Velocities are derived. They were computed from the tracked positions, so "rate of change of position = velocity" in the full model confirms the preprocessing, not the physics.
  • Time scale. Times were taken from the frame rate stored in the video file (50.065 frames per second); the camera's stated rate is 50. The rates above are therefore about 0.26% too high.

How it was made

  1. Track. Four starting points were picked by hand in the first frame (the tops of the two bobs and the two knots) and followed through the video with Lucas–Kanade optical flow (DUSTrack).
  2. Add velocities. A smoothing derivative of each coordinate, in pixels per second.
  3. Discover. eqdisc-agent was run once on the 16 columns, named only p1…p4. It was given no description of what they are. From the numbers alone it concluded they were tracks of two coupled pendulums.
  4. Simulate and draw. The submitted equations were integrated from the first frame and drawn over the original footage.

The discovery run made 23 tool calls and cost $3.61. An earlier run on positions without velocities could not find any dynamics, because a swinging system cannot be written in positions alone.
